The highest court has ruled that digital communications require a warrant for law enforcement access, reshaping privacy law for the modern era. The 6-3 decision establishes that the Fourth Amendment's protections against unreasonable search and seizure extend fully to digital communications stored by third-party service providers.

The ruling overturns a 1979 precedent that had formed the legal foundation for warrantless access to digital records. Writing for the majority, the Chief Justice argued that "the digital exhaust of modern life reveals far more about a person than any physical search could uncover."

The case originated from a challenge to the government's warrantless access to six months of location data from a suspect's mobile phone provider. Privacy advocates hailed the decision as the most significant digital rights ruling in a decade.

Justice departments across the country will now need to adjust investigative procedures, potentially slowing certain types of digital investigations. Law enforcement agencies expressed concern that the ruling could hinder counterterrorism and criminal investigations.
